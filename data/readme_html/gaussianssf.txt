<p dir="auto">This package provides a simulation method for calculating the quantum dynamics of a multimode, one-dimensional field, subject to dispersion and nonlinearity.
The prototypical use case is nonlinear propagation of an ultrafast optical pulse in a waveguided medium.
However, the most general setup is any Heisenberg equation of motion of the form</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$
\mathrm{i} \partial_z \hat\Psi^{(i)}_x(z) = F^{(i)}(z,\hat\Psi_x(z)) + D^{(i)}(-\mathrm{i}\partial_x) \hat\Psi^{(i)}_x(z),
$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">where, for each <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$i$</math-renderer>, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi_x^{(i)}$</math-renderer> is a vector-valued bosonic field operator defined on a grid with coordinate <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$x$</math-renderer>. The field components <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi^{(i)}_x$</math-renderer> satisfy</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$
\left[ \hat\Psi_{x}^{(i)}, \hat\Psi_{y}^{(j)\dagger} \right] = \delta_{ij} \delta(x-y).
$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">In an optical system, the index <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$i$</math-renderer> can, e.g., enumerate different pulse envelopes (e.g., fundamental- vs. second-harmonic), whereas <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$x$</math-renderer> would be the pulse coordinate (e.g., in femtoseconds).</p>
<p dir="auto">Here, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$F^{(i)}$</math-renderer> are any sufficiently well-behaved (nonlinear) functions (one for each field) that can depend on both the position and the field, and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$D^{(i)}(-\mathrm{i}\partial_x)$</math-renderer> are linear operators acting on each field representing the effect of linear dispersion.
In this generic setting, we use <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$z$</math-renderer> to denote the coordinate along which <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi_x$</math-renderer> evolves; we henceforth suppress the <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$z$</math-renderer> dependence of our field.</p>
<p dir="auto">In the classical theory (i.e., when <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi_x$</math-renderer> is replaced by a c-number function of <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$x$</math-renderer>), an efficient way to solve the dynamics is to use a split-step Fourier (SSF) method, in which the first term is applied on the "real space" grid <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$x$</math-renderer> while the second term is applied in the "reciprocal space" of <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$x$</math-renderer>; in reciprocal space with coordinate <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\xi$</math-renderer>, the action of <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$D^{(i)}$</math-renderer> becomes simply <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$D^{(i)}(2\pi\xi)\hat\Psi^{(i)}_\xi$</math-renderer>, acting on the Fourier transform of <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi_x$</math-renderer>.
In this package, we provide functionality for extending such SSF methods to capture leading-order quantum effects.</p>
<p dir="auto">More specifically, this package focuses on capturing second-order correlations in the quantum state via a <em>Gaussian-state approximation</em>, in which we derive equations of motion for both the mean-field components</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$\Psi^{(i)}_x = \langle\hat\Psi^{(i)}_x\rangle$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">as well as the covariance matrix elements</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$
\begin{align}
\Sigma_{x,y}^{(i,j)} &amp;= \langle \delta\hat\Psi_x^{(i)} \delta\hat\Psi_y^{(j)}\rangle \\
\Pi_{x,y}^{(i,j)} &amp;= \langle \delta\hat\Psi_x^{(i)\dagger} \delta\hat\Psi_y^{(j)}\rangle,
\end{align}
$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">where <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\delta\hat\Psi_x = \hat\Psi_x - \langle \hat\Psi_x \rangle$</math-renderer> is the fluctuation operator corresponding to <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi_x$</math-renderer>. The mean-field components correspond to the usual classical field (with dynamical corrections), while the covariance matrices capture the statistics of quantum fluctuations and correlations.</p>
<p dir="auto">Under appropriate conditions, we can formulate a Gaussian-state approximation of the dynamics in the generic form</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$
\begin{align}
\mathrm{i} \partial_z \Psi_x^{(i)} &amp;= G^{(i)}(\Psi_x,\Sigma_{x,x},\Pi_{x,x}) + D^{(i)}(-\mathrm{i}\partial_x) \Psi_x^{(i)}, \\
\mathrm{i} \partial_z \Sigma_{x,y}^{(i,j)} &amp;= G_\Sigma^{(i,j)}(\Psi_x,\Psi_y,\Sigma_{x,y},\Pi_{x,y}) + D^{(i)}(-\mathrm{i}\partial_x) D^{(j)}(-\mathrm{i}\partial_y) \Sigma_{x,y}^{(i,j)}, \\
\mathrm{i} \partial_z \Pi_{x,y}^{(i,j)} &amp;= G_\Pi^{(i,j)}(\Psi_x,\Psi_y,\Sigma_{x,y},\Pi_{x,y}) + D^{(i)}(+\mathrm{i}\partial_x) D^{(j)}(-\mathrm{i}\partial_y) \Pi_{x,y}^{(i,j)},
\end{align}
$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">for appropriate functions <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G$</math-renderer>, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Sigma$</math-renderer>, and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Pi$</math-renderer>, which can be derived from <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$F$</math-renderer>.
This set of equations describe the (nonlinear) evolution of the Gaussian moments of the state. Our numerical approach solves these Gaussian-moment equations using a split-step Fourier method, hence the name of the package GaussianSSF.jl.</p>

<p dir="auto">Define the functions <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$D^{(i)}$</math-renderer> via a tuple of callables <code>D_funcs = (D1, D2, ...)</code> which can be evaluated on <code>ξ</code> (as defined above) to give the correct propagation constant for the corresponding field at the reciprocal-space coordinate <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\xi$</math-renderer>.
Note that this propagation constant can be complex-valued, with the imaginary part representing loss.</p>
<p dir="auto">In general, the above construction allows you to build your own propagation constants for arbitrary dispersion models, but for simple situations, the package provides a convenience dispersion model <code>taylor</code>:</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="D = taylor(β), # or taylor(β...)
D_funcs = (D,)"><pre class="notranslate"><code>D = taylor(β), # or taylor(β...)
D_funcs = (D,)
</code></pre></div>
<p dir="auto">will produce a Taylor-expanded dispersion model for a single field, which acts in reciprocal space as</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$
D(2\pi\xi) = \sum_{j=0}^{|\beta|-1} \frac{\beta_{j}}{j!} , (2\pi\xi)^j
$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">where <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$|\beta|$</math-renderer> is <code>length(β)</code> and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\beta_j$</math-renderer> is <code>β[j+1]</code>. (Note that <code>taylor()</code> is also provided as a shorthand for <code>taylor(0,0,1)</code>.)</p>

<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h3 class="heading-element" dir="auto">4. Specify integration method</h3><a id="user-content-4-specify-integration-method" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: 4. Specify integration method" href="#4-specify-integration-method"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">Instantiate an <code>Integrator</code> to represent an integration method for stepping the solution forward along <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$z$</math-renderer>. Currently, this package has implemented RK4IP (a fourth order Runge-Kutta method in the interaction picture), which is a fixed-step method. To signify that we want to use such a method with a time step <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\mathrm{d}z$</math-renderer>, we can use</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="stepper = RK4IP(dz)"><pre class="notranslate"><code>stepper = RK4IP(dz)
</code></pre></div>
<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h3 class="heading-element" dir="auto">5. Create simulation object</h3><a id="user-content-5-create-simulation-object" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: 5. Create simulation object" href="#5-create-simulation-object"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">Instantiate a <code>GSSFSim</code> object using all of the above information according to</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="sim = GSSFSim(stepper, model, N_grid, X_window, D_funcs)"><pre class="notranslate"><code>sim = GSSFSim(stepper, model, N_grid, X_window, D_funcs)
</code></pre></div>
<p dir="auto">The object <code>sim</code> contains all the data (states, linear operators, etc.) and background/cached data structures needed to <em>efficiently</em> step through the integration procedure.</p>
<p dir="auto">The most useful field in <code>GSSFSim</code> is the <em>state</em> of the pulse, which accessed via <code>sim.state</code>. By default, for a quantum field with <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$M$</math-renderer> component, this produces a tuple representing all the Gaussian moments in the following order:</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$
\left(\psi^{(1)}, \ldots, \psi^{(M)},
\Sigma^{(1,1)}, \Pi^{(1,1)},
\ldots,
\Sigma^{(1,M)}, \Pi^{(1,M)},
\Sigma^{(2,2)}, \Pi^{(2,2)},
\ldots,
\Sigma^{(2,M)}, \Pi^{(2,M)},
\ldots,
\Sigma^{(M,M)}, \Pi^{(M,M)}
\right)
$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">However, this default behavior can be modified (e.g., if we wish to neglect certain Gaussian moments or know certain moments are zero by symmetry). This can be done by implementing your own method for the function <code>linear_setup</code> on different subtypes of <code>Model</code>: See more information under <a href="#implementing-new-models">Implementing new models</a>.</p>
<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h3 class="heading-element" dir="auto">6. State initialization</h3><a id="user-content-6-state-initialization" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: 6. State initialization" href="#6-state-initialization"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">Initialize the state by calling <code>init_state!</code> on <code>sim</code> according to</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="init_state!(sim, init_funcs...)"><pre class="notranslate"><code>init_state!(sim, init_funcs...)
</code></pre></div>
<p dir="auto">where each element <code>sim.state[i]</code> is filled by evaluating <code>init_funcs[i]</code> on <code>x</code> (if <code>sim.state[i]</code> is a vector) or on the meshgrid of <code>x</code> (if <code>sim.state[i]</code> is a matrix).
Here, <code>length(init_funcs)</code> can be smaller than <code>length(sim.state)</code>, in which case the following elements of <code>sim.state</code> are not modified.</p>
<p dir="auto">For the <a href="##monte-carlo-classical-trajectories">specialized model <code>ParallelizedMC</code></a> which runs parallel Monte-Carlo simulations of classical trajectories with different initial conditions, multiple dispatch on <code>ParallelizedMC</code> has been defined to also add random noise to the background to simulate the effects of quantum noise.</p>
<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h3 class="heading-element" dir="auto">7. Run simulation</h3><a id="user-content-7-run-simulation" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: 7. Run simulation" href="#7-run-simulation"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">Run the simulation! This can be done either by calling</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="step!(sim, z)"><pre class="notranslate"><code>step!(sim, z)
</code></pre></div>
<p dir="auto">which takes one step of the <code>Integrator</code>, or by calling</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="gssf!(sim, N_steps], N_save=1, save_fun!=Array.(sim.state))"><pre class="notranslate"><code>gssf!(sim, N_steps], N_save=1, save_fun!=Array.(sim.state))
</code></pre></div>
<p dir="auto">which repeats <code>step!</code> for <code>N_steps</code> iterations starting from <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$z = 0$</math-renderer> (and, for <code>RK4IP</code> as the stepper, in steps of <code>dz</code>).
Furthermore, whenever <code>div(N_steps, N_save) == 0</code> evaluates true, the result of <code>save_fun!(sim)</code> is appended to a data structure <code>output</code>.
By default, <code>save_fun!</code> simply converts the entirety of <code>sim.state</code> into a tuple of <code>Array</code>s (from <code>CuArray</code>s).
At the end of the iteration, <code>output</code> is returned along with a corresponding vector <code>z</code> describing the value of <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$z$</math-renderer> at which the elements of output were obtained.</p>

<p dir="auto">Finally, one can analyze the simulation results. This can either be done by looking at <code>sim.state</code> which provides the full Gaussian approximation of the quantum state at the end of the simulation (note that this is still stored as a tuple of <code>CuArray</code>s!), or by looking at <code>output</code> if <code>gssf!</code> was used.</p>

<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h2 class="heading-element" dir="auto">Nonlinear Schroedinger equation (NLSE)</h2><a id="user-content-nonlinear-schroedinger-equation-nlse" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: Nonlinear Schroedinger equation (NLSE)" href="#nonlinear-schroedinger-equation-nlse"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">Implemented by <code>NLSE &lt;: Model</code>.</p>
<p dir="auto">Let <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi_x$</math-renderer> be a single-component field, so that we can drop the superscript indices. For a real coupling parameter <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$g$</math-renderer>,</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$
F(z, \hat\Psi_x) = g \hat\Psi_x^{\dagger2} \hat\Psi_x^2.
$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">In an optical system, this describes 4-wave mixing nonlinearity.
The resulting <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G$</math-renderer>, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Sigma$</math-renderer> and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Pi$</math-renderer> functions are derived in [<a href="#gssfpaper">1</a>] and implemented here.</p>
<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h2 class="heading-element" dir="auto">Linearized NLSE</h2><a id="user-content-linearized-nlse" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: Linearized NLSE" href="#linearized-nlse"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">Implemented by <code>Linearized{NLSE} &lt;: DerivedModel &lt;: Model</code>.</p>
<p dir="auto">A common simplification made to NLSE dynamics is to assume that the quantum noise does not participate in nonlinear dynamics.
This can be implemented mathematically by removing all terms that are nonlinear in covariance terms.
That is, we assume that <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\langle\hat\Phi_x\rangle$</math-renderer> is order unity, while <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\langle\hat\Phi_y\hat\Phi_x\rangle \sim \langle\hat\Phi_y^\dagger\hat\Phi_x\rangle \sim O(\varepsilon)$</math-renderer>, and we only keep terms in the equations of motion up to <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$O(\varepsilon)$</math-renderer>.
This effectively simplifies the form of the <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G$</math-renderer>, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Sigma$</math-renderer> and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Pi$</math-renderer> functions.</p>
<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h2 class="heading-element" dir="auto">Quasi-degenerate 3-wave mixing (QD3WM)</h2><a id="user-content-quasi-degenerate-3-wave-mixing-qd3wm" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: Quasi-degenerate 3-wave mixing (QD3WM)" href="#quasi-degenerate-3-wave-mixing-qd3wm"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">Implemented by <code>QD3WM &lt;: Model</code>.</p>
<p dir="auto">Let <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi_x^{(1)} = \hat\Phi_x$</math-renderer> and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi_x^{(2)} = \hat\Psi_x$</math-renderer>. For a real coupling parameter <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\epsilon$</math-renderer>,</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$
\begin{align}
F^{(1)}(z, \hat\Phi_x, \hat\Psi_x) &amp;= \mathrm{i}\epsilon \hat\Psi_x \Phi^\dagger_x \\
F^{(2)}(z, \hat\Phi_x, \hat\Psi_x) &amp;= -\frac{\mathrm{i}\epsilon}{2} \Phi^2_x.
\end{align}
$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">In an optical system, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Phi_x$</math-renderer> decribes a fundamental envelope while <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi_x$</math-renderer> describes the corresponding second-harmonic envelope, and this model describes 3-wave mixing, e.g., between pump in the former and signal/idler in the latter.
The resulting <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G$</math-renderer>, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Sigma$</math-renderer> and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Pi$</math-renderer> functions are derived in [<a href="#gssfpaper">1</a>] and implemented here.</p>
<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h2 class="heading-element" dir="auto">Vacuum-squeezing QD3WM</h2><a id="user-content-vacuum-squeezing-qd3wm" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: Vacuum-squeezing QD3WM" href="#vacuum-squeezing-qd3wm"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">Implemented by <code>Squeezing{QD3WM} &lt;: DerivedModel &lt;: Model</code>.</p>
<p dir="auto">For QD3WM, if the initial condition of the signal <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Phi_x$</math-renderer> is assumed to be vacuum and the initial condition of the pump <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat\Psi_x$</math-renderer> is assumed to be a coherent state, then by symmetry of the QD3WM system, we have the significant simplification that</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$
\langle\hat\Phi_x\rangle = \langle\hat\Psi_y\hat\Phi_x \rangle = \langle\hat\Psi_y^\dagger\hat\Phi_x \rangle = \langle\hat\Psi_y\hat\Psi_x\rangle = \langle\hat\Psi_y^\dagger\hat\Psi_x\rangle = 0.
$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">which implies that we can eliminate the Gaussian moments <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\Psi_x$</math-renderer>, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\Sigma^{(1,2)}$</math-renderer>, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\Pi^{(1,2)}$</math-renderer>, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\Sigma^{(2,2)}$</math-renderer>, and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\Pi^{(2,2)}$</math-renderer>, resulting in 1 fewer 1D FFT and 4 fewer 2D FFTs.
In addition, we no longer need to define the corresponding nonlinear functions $G^{(1,2)}<em>\Sigma$, $G^{(1,2)}</em>\Pi$, $G^{(2,2)}<em>\Sigma$, and $G^{(2,2)}</em>\Pi$.
The remaining <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G$</math-renderer>, <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Sigma$</math-renderer> and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Pi$</math-renderer> functions are derived in [<a href="#gssfpaper">1</a>] and implemented here.</p>
<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h2 class="heading-element" dir="auto">Linearized vacuum-squeezing QD3WM</h2><a id="user-content-linearized-vacuum-squeezing-qd3wm" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: Linearized vacuum-squeezing QD3WM" href="#linearized-vacuum-squeezing-qd3wm"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">Implemented by <code>Linearized{Squeezing{QD3WM}} &lt;: DerivedModel &lt;: Model</code>.</p>
<p dir="auto">A further approximation can be made by assuming that all the dynamics are linear: This is physically equivalent to an undepleted-pump approximation for vacuum squeezing in QD3WM.
In this case, we have some additional simplifications in the nonlinear functions: <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G^{(2)} = 0$</math-renderer>, while <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Sigma^{(1,1)}$</math-renderer> and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Pi^{(1,1)}$</math-renderer> are further simplified.</p>
<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h2 class="heading-element" dir="auto">Classical QD3WM</h2><a id="user-content-classical-qd3wm" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: Classical QD3WM" href="#classical-qd3wm"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">On the extreme end, we can fully classicalize the QD3WM model, which assumes that a coherent state for all fields throughout the evolution. That is, all elements <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\Sigma = \Pi = 0$</math-renderer>, so we also have <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G_\Sigma = G_\Pi = 0$</math-renderer>.
In this case, we also set <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$G(\Phi_x, \Psi_x) = F(\Phi_x, \Psi_x)$</math-renderer> (dropping hats on the arguments to <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$F$</math-renderer>).</p>
<div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h2 class="heading-element" dir="auto">Monte-Carlo classical trajectories</h2><a id="user-content-monte-carlo-classical-trajectories" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: Monte-Carlo classical trajectories" href="#monte-carlo-classical-trajectories"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">Implemented by <code>ParallelMC{Classical{&lt;:Model}} &lt;: DerivedModel &lt;: Model</code>.</p>
<p dir="auto">This is a specialized model which is meant to run many classical trajectories in parallel, seeded by random noise in the initial condtion. (This is done by dispatching on the function <code>init_state!</code>.)
The equations of motion are exactly the same as those for its underlying <code>Classical</code> model.
However, the state of the simulation is structured as a matrix whose columns represent different trajectories, and the linear step instead performs parallel 1D FFTs along the columns.</p>

<p dir="auto">It is a central goal of this package to be as generic as possible. For that reason, in addition to the models implemented above, we also need an extensible framework for adding custom models, corresponding to custom coupled-wave equations. <div class="markdown-heading" dir="auto"><h2 class="heading-element" dir="auto">Pulsed vacuum squeezing</h2><a id="user-content-pulsed-vacuum-squeezing" class="anchor" aria-label="Permalink: Pulsed vacuum squeezing" href="#pulsed-vacuum-squeezing"><svg class="octicon octicon-link" viewBox="0 0 16 16" version="1.1" width="16" height="16" aria-hidden="true"><path d="m7.775 3.275 1.25-1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1 4.95 4.95l-2.5 2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1-4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1 .018-1.042.751.751 0 0 1 1.042-.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l2.5-2.5a2.002 2.002 0 0 0-2.83-2.83l-1.25 1.25a.751.751 0 0 1-1.042-.018.751.751 0 0 1-.018-1.042Zm-4.69 9.64a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 2.83 0l1.25-1.25a.751.751 0 0 1 1.042.018.751.751 0 0 1 .018 1.042l-1.25 1.25a3.5 3.5 0 1 1-4.95-4.95l2.5-2.5a3.5 3.5 0 0 1 4.95 0 .751.751 0 0 1-.018 1.042.751.751 0 0 1-1.042.018 1.998 1.998 0 0 0-2.83 0l-2.5 2.5a1.998 1.998 0 0 0 0 2.83Z"></path></svg></a></div>
<p dir="auto">We consider vacuum squeezing on a <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\chi^{(2)}$</math-renderer>-nonlinear waveguide pumped by a broadband pulse. For the simulation, we first set the number of grids and the size of the simulation window:</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="N_grid = 2^8
X_window = 10.0

x, dx = realspace(N_grid, X_window)
ξ, dξ = wavespace(N_grid, X_window)"><pre class="notranslate"><code>N_grid = 2^8
X_window = 10.0

x, dx = realspace(N_grid, X_window)
ξ, dξ = wavespace(N_grid, X_window)
</code></pre></div>
<p dir="auto">The three-wave mixing interaction on a <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\chi^{(2)}$</math-renderer> waveguide involves fundamental harmonics and second harmonics with field operators <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat{\Psi}_x^{(1)}(z)$</math-renderer> and <math-renderer class="js-inline-math" style="display: inline-block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$\hat{\Psi}_x^{(2)}(z)$</math-renderer>, respectively. The nonlinear part of the coupled-wave equation takes a form</p>
<p dir="auto"><math-renderer class="js-display-math" style="display: block" data-static-url="https://github.githubassets.com/static" data-run-id="430c13a7cf1d3e3de83a01f4f33a9490">$$
\begin{align}
F^{(1)}(z,\hat{\Psi}_x(z))&amp;=\epsilon \hat\Psi_x^{(1)\dagger}(z)\hat\Psi_x^{(2)}(z) \\
F^{(2)}(z,\hat{\Psi}_x(z))&amp;=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\hat\Psi_x^{(1)2}(z),
\end{align}
$$</math-renderer></p>
<p dir="auto">which can be defined via the code</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="ϵ = 1.0
model = QD3WM(ϵ)"><pre class="notranslate"><code>ϵ = 1.0
model = QD3WM(ϵ)
</code></pre></div>
<p dir="auto">For the linear part of the dynamics, we can define the linear operators as</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="β1_2 = 1.0

</code></pre></div>
<p dir="auto">for instance, where <code>κ1</code> and <code>κ2</code> are the loss rates of FH and SH, respectively, and we have assumed zero phase- and group-velocity mismatch.</p>
<p dir="auto">The functions <code>model</code>, <code>D1</code> and <code>D2</code> characterizes the structure of the coupled-wave equations on the analytic level. For practical numerical simulations, we also need to specify the total propagation time <code>Z</code>, the number of time steps <code>N_steps</code>, and the number of time slices to save the data <code>N_save</code>. These system parameters can be specified as</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="Z = 0.5

<p dir="auto">where <code>sim</code> object defines an RK4 integrator to solve the coupled-wave equations.</p>
<p dir="auto">Finally, we specify the initial condition for the simulation <code>init</code>. As an example, we consider an initial coherent Gaussian pump pulse with width <code>σz</code> and peak amplitude <code>β0</code> as</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="σx = 0.5

<p dir="auto">where the initial signal state is a vacuum.</p>
<p dir="auto">Now, we are ready to run the simulation by a line of code</p>
<div class="snippet-clipboard-content notranslate position-relative overflow-auto" data-snippet-clipboard-copy-content="output, zout = gssf!(sim, N_steps, N_save)"><pre class="notranslate"><code>output, zout = gssf!(sim, N_steps, N_save)
</code></pre></div>
<p dir="auto">The output contains the mean-field and covariance matrices of the fields. By calculating the eigenvalues of the signal covariance matrix, we obtain pulse quadratures that are squeezed/anti-squeezed.

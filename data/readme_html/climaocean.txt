<p dir="auto"><code>ClimaOcean</code> is for <em>realistic</em> ocean-only and ocean + sea-ice simulations, in a region of the ocean ("regional") or covering the whole Earth.
<a href="https://github.com/CliMA/Oceananigans.jl">Oceananigans</a> is a lower-level package for simulating the dynamics of ocean-flavored fluids that can be used for <em>both</em> idealized problems and, given enough effort, realistic problems as well.
While "idealized" problems come in multifarious shapes and sizes, "realistic" problems tend to be more narrowly defined, and require</p>
<ul dir="auto">
<li>Simulating the evolution of specific tracers: ocean temperature (or heat), salinity, and sometimes ocean biogeochemistry.</li>
<li>Computing fluxes of heat, water vapor, momentum, and trace gases between the ocean and atmosphere (where the atmospheric state is either prescribed or "coupled" and itself evolving) -- and also between sea ice and the atmosphere, when a sea ice component is included.</li>
<li>Initializing the ocean model with realistic initial conditions derived from observations of the ocean, and realistic bathymetry.</li>
</ul>
<p dir="auto"><code>ClimaOcean</code> leverages <code>Oceananigans</code> and <code>ClimaSeaIce</code> to build <code>OceanSeaIceModel</code>s capable of meeting these requirements to simulate the dynamics of specific regions of the Earth's ocean.
So if you're using <code>ClimaOcean</code>, it's a very good idea to become proficient in <a href="https://github.com/CliMA/Oceananigans.jl"><code>Oceananigans</code></a> as well.
Note also that, at least at the moment, <code>ClimaOcean</code> is focused on hydrostatic modeling with <code>Oceananigans</code>' <code>HydrostaticFreeSurfaceModel</code>.</p>
<p dir="auto">In summary, if you're interested in realistic, hydrostatic regional or global simulations you may find <code>ClimaOcean</code> useful.
Otherwise, you can stick with <a href="https://github.com/CliMA/Oceananigans.jl">Oceananigans</a>.</p>
